Carrot & Stick: Why Building More Doesn't Always Lower Rents, and What NYC Can Do About It

New research on how housing incentives and affordability mandates actually work — and what each policy costs, and who pays.

Daniel Lebret, Crocker H. Liu & Maxence Valentin · Cornell University & ETH Zurich · 2026

01 · THE PUZZLE

Housing production is up. So are rents. Why?

New York City has added tens of thousands of housing units over the past decade. Yet rents have kept climbing, and affordability pressures show no sign of easing. The standard explanation — that more supply should lower prices — seems to be failing.

It isn't failing. It's just incomplete.

In mature cities like New York, housing supply doesn't work the way introductory economics suggests. There is no empty margin to build on. Instead, new construction means replacing what's already there — tearing down older, depreciated buildings and putting up newer, taller ones. That replacement process changes not just how many units exist, but what kinds of units they are. And that distinction turns out to matter enormously for rents, affordability, and policy design.

02 · THE MECHANISM

Replacement, not expansion

When a developer tears down a rent-discounted walk-up in Bushwick and builds a new 12-story building, something that looks like a supply increase is also a composition shift. The new units enter the market without accumulated depreciation, at higher rents than what they replaced. More units, but higher average rents.

We call this the replacement effect — and in New York City, it systematically offsets the downward pressure on rents that additional supply would otherwise produce.

This doesn't mean building is bad. It means the policy question is more specific than 'build more or build less.' The right questions are: which parcels get redeveloped, when, at what density, and under what conditions. Those are exactly the levers that housing policy controls — and exactly what this research quantifies.

03 · CARROTS

Tax exemptions and density bonuses — not interchangeable

NYC's housing toolkit includes two main types of incentives: property tax exemptions (like the former 421-a program) and density bonuses (upzoning and floor-area ratio increases). Both expand housing supply. They do not work the same way.

Tax exemptions work on timing. By reducing post-construction tax liability, they make redevelopment profitable sooner — pulling forward projects that would otherwise sit on the margin for years. The effect is broad: it raises redevelopment probabilities across a wide range of parcels, concentrating new construction in areas with older buildings and significant unused density. Western Brooklyn and Queens respond most strongly. The tradeoff is fiscal: the city foregoes substantial tax revenue during the exemption window.

Density bonuses work on scale. By allowing taller buildings, they increase the value of redevelopment — but they also increase the value of waiting. A developer who can build 15 stories instead of 10 may rationally hold off until rents are high enough to justify the larger project. The result is that density bonuses often delay construction on parcels near the redevelopment threshold, even as they increase the size of projects that do eventually get built. High-value locations benefit most; marginal parcels may actually see lower near-term redevelopment probabilities.

Tax exemptions and density bonuses are not substitutes. A city that relies on only one is leaving a meaningful lever untouched. Stacking both tools produces the largest supply expansion — accelerating redevelopment while also increasing density conditional on redevelopment.

The spatial contrast is stark: tax exemptions generate broad redevelopment gains across older neighborhoods citywide, while upzoning concentrates gains in a narrower set of high-capacity locations. A planner choosing between these tools is making a choice about which neighborhoods benefit — not just how much gets built.

04 · STICKS

What affordability mandates actually deliver

Affordable housing mandates — requirements that a share of units in new construction be rented below market rate — are the most direct tool for producing affordable units. They are also the most widely misunderstood.

What mandates accomplish: They reallocate access to new construction. When a mandate requires 30% of units in a new building to be affordable, those units are genuinely below market rate — and they genuinely reach households who would otherwise be priced out of new construction entirely.

What mandates don't accomplish: They don't expand the bottom of the rent distribution in the way policymakers often hope. The affordable units produced through mandates tend to appear in new buildings on higher-value parcels — because those are the projects where redevelopment still pencils out despite the affordability requirement. The lowest-rent housing in NYC continues to be supplied by the existing aging stock, not by mandated units in new construction.

Mandates also reduce the net return to redevelopment. Developers on marginal parcels delay or forgo projects when a mandate is imposed. The result is fewer total units than would otherwise be built, with the reduction concentrated in market-rate units. This is why mandates work best when paired with incentives — the carrots offset the supply-dampening effect of the stick.

05 · THE COST

Who pays for each mandated affordable unit?

Because below-market housing is not privately profitable, affordability mandates create a gap between private returns and public objectives. Someone has to absorb that gap. Our analysis makes explicit who that is — and how the answer depends entirely on which policy instrument is used.

Each mandated affordable unit carries an approximate cost of $65,000 in foregone value, split between private landowners and the public sector. But the split depends on the instrument.

Under tax exemptions paired with mandates, the cost falls almost entirely on the public sector — through foregone property tax revenues during the exemption window. Landowners are largely held harmless. The city bears the burden.

Under density bonuses paired with mandates, the cost is shared more evenly. Landowners absorb roughly half through lower land value capitalization; the public sector absorbs the rest. Neither side escapes unscathed, but neither bears the full cost alone.

Fiscal instruments are politically easier to pass — landowners support them — but expensive for city budgets. Regulatory instruments preserve public revenues but face stronger opposition from property owners. Understanding this tradeoff is essential for designing the next generation of NYC affordable housing programs.

06 · POLICY IMPLICATIONS

Three takeaways for program design

01

Stack the tools

The largest supply gains — in both market-rate and affordable units — come from combining tax exemptions, density bonuses, and affordability mandates. Each instrument operates on a different margin of the redevelopment decision. Using all three simultaneously produces outcomes no single instrument can achieve alone.

02

Design for the margin

Policy effects are not uniform across the city. Parcels near their redevelopment threshold respond strongly to tax incentives but weakly to density bonuses. High-value locations respond to density bonuses but are less sensitive to fiscal incentives. Effective program design targets the right instrument at the right parcel type.

03

Don't expect mandates to reach the lowest-rent households

Affordable units produced through inclusionary requirements tend to cluster in new buildings on higher-value sites — above the bottom quintile of the rent distribution. Reaching the lowest-income households requires preserving and improving the existing aging stock, not just mandating affordability in new construction.

07 · WHERE TO TARGET

Not all neighborhoods are the same

Not all neighborhoods are the same, and not all policy tools work equally well everywhere. We evaluate the same three mandatory Carrot & Stick policy bundles — Density Incentive Zoning, Fiscal Incentive Zoning, and All Policies Combined — applied exclusively to three distinct groups of parcels in NYC. The results show that targeting strategy matters as much as policy design.

High-Demand Parcels

Manhattan · Western Brooklyn · Western Queens

Targeting the top 20% of parcels by rent potential delivers the largest absolute gains in housing production — but also the strongest upward pressure on rents, reflecting the replacement effect at its most intense.

Policy Net New Units Affordable Units Rent Change Tax Revenue
Density Incentive +31,430 +41,117 +0.36% -0.05%
Fiscal Incentive +33,115 +48,278 +0.53% -1.86%
All Policies +120,729 +92,952 +2.58% -2.96%

Targeting high-demand areas maximizes unit production but generates the strongest replacement-driven rent increases. The fiscal cost is also highest here.

High-Obsolescence Parcels

Brooklyn · Bronx · Queens

Targeting the top 20% of parcels by building age — the most physically deteriorated stock — produces more modest unit gains but the smallest rent increases of any targeting strategy. Replacing the worst buildings moderates the replacement effect.

Policy Net New Units Affordable Units Rent Change Tax Revenue
Density Incentive +11,707 +15,577 +0.14% -0.09%
Fiscal Incentive +17,577 +22,343 +0.27% -0.69%
All Policies +58,401 +41,768 +1.15% -1.25%

Targeting obsolete buildings moderates the replacement effect — new construction replaces the lowest-quality stock, compressing the quality gap between old and new. Rent increases are roughly half those seen in high-demand areas under equivalent policies.

High Unused-Capacity Parcels

Bronx · Staten Island · Eastern Queens

Targeting the bottom 20% of parcels by capacity utilization — those with the most unused floor-area ratio — produces strong unit gains. Critically, this is the only targeting scenario where supply expansion actually reduces expected rents.

Policy Net New Units Affordable Units Rent Change Tax Revenue
Density Incentive +18,756 +49,139 -0.26% -0.32%
Fiscal Incentive +53,165 +68,306 -0.07% -2.98%
All Policies +105,284 +84,485 +0.25% -3.67%

In lower-demand areas with substantial unused capacity, the traditional supply mechanism works as expected — more units exert genuine downward pressure on rents. This is the only scenario where building more lowers average rents.

The same policy bundle produces twice as many units when targeted at high-demand parcels compared to high-obsolescence parcels — but at the cost of stronger rent increases and higher fiscal burden. Only in lower-demand, high-capacity areas does supply expansion reduce average rents. Effective housing policy requires matching the instrument to the neighborhood, not applying a single program citywide.
